What is ads.txt?

ads.txt (Authorized Digital Sellers) is a plain-text file that publishers host at the root of their domain — for example, example.com/ads.txt — to publicly declare which companies are authorized to sell their digital advertising inventory. It was created by the IAB Tech Lab and officially launched in 2017 as a simple, open mechanism to combat domain spoofing and supply-chain fraud in programmatic advertising.

Before ads.txt, fraudulent sellers could claim to represent premium publishers in bid requests with no way for buyers to verify the claim. ads.txt solves this by giving publishers direct authorship over a canonical list of their approved supply partners. Because the file lives on the publisher's actual web server, only someone with legitimate access to that domain can modify it — making it a trusted, tamper-resistant declaration.

As of 2025, ads.txt compliance is required or strongly enforced by virtually every major DSP, including Google DV360, The Trade Desk, Amazon DSP, and Xandr. Publishers without a valid ads.txt file face reduced fill rates, lower CPMs, and in some cases complete exclusion from programmatic demand.

IAB Tech Lab Specification

The full ads.txt specification is maintained by IAB Tech Lab at iabtechlab.com/ads-txt/. AdsDB tracks real-world implementation across 41,856+ live publisher domains, giving you empirical data on how publishers actually deploy ads.txt in the wild.

The ads.txt File Format

An ads.txt file is a plain UTF-8 text document served with MIME type text/plain. Each non-comment line contains exactly three required fields and one optional field, separated by commas. Lines beginning with # are treated as comments and ignored by parsers.

Canonical Example

# ads.txt for example.com
# Format: Ad System Domain, Publisher Account ID, Relationship, Certification Authority ID

google.com, pub-1234567890123456, DIRECT, f08c47fec0942fa0
appnexus.com, 7118, DIRECT, f5ab79cb980f11d1
pubmatic.com, 158193, DIRECT, 5d62403b186f2ace
openx.com, 540338152, RESELLER, 6a698e965929636
rubiconproject.com, 17960, RESELLER, 0bfd66d529a55807
criteo.com, B-062114, RESELLER

# Variable declarations
OWNERDOMAIN=example.com
SUBDOMAIN=ads.example.com

Field-by-Field Specification

Field # Name Required Description Example
1 Ad System Domain Required The canonical domain of the advertising system (SSP, exchange, or ad network) authorized to sell this publisher's inventory. Must match the domain used in that system's sellers.json file. google.com
2 Publisher Account ID Required The publisher's account identifier within the ad system listed in Field 1. This is the seller_id that will appear in the corresponding sellers.json entry. Case-sensitive. pub-1234567890123456
3 Relationship Type Required Either DIRECT or RESELLER. DIRECT indicates a direct contractual relationship; RESELLER indicates authorized resale through another party. DIRECT
4 Certification Authority ID Optional The TAG-ID (Trustworthy Accountability Group identifier) of the advertising system. Enables cryptographic verification of the seller's identity via the TAG registry. f08c47fec0942fa0

Variable Declarations

In addition to seller lines, ads.txt supports special variable declarations that provide additional context about the publisher's supply chain configuration:

Variable Description Example
OWNERDOMAIN Declares the root domain of the content owner when using subdomains or when a CMS manages ads.txt on behalf of multiple sites. OWNERDOMAIN=example.com
SUBDOMAIN Indicates a subdomain that also has an ads.txt file. Crawlers should fetch ads.txt from the declared subdomain as well. SUBDOMAIN=blog.example.com
CONTACT An email address or URL where buyers can reach the publisher with questions about the ads.txt file. CONTACT=adops@example.com

DIRECT vs RESELLER

The relationship type field is arguably the most important field in ads.txt. It tells buyers exactly how the listed seller fits into the publisher's supply chain, and significantly affects bid competition, CPMs, and fraud risk.

DIRECT

A DIRECT entry means the publisher has a direct contractual relationship with the advertising system listed in Field 1. The publisher signed up for an account with that SSP or exchange themselves, accepted their terms of service, and receives payment directly from them. There are no intermediaries between the publisher and the listed ad system.

Why DIRECT commands higher CPMs

DSPs and buyers strongly prefer DIRECT inventory because it has a shorter, more auditable supply chain. Fewer hops mean fewer fees deducted, and the buyer's dollar reaches the publisher more efficiently. Most quality-focused DSPs apply bid multipliers that favor DIRECT inventory over RESELLER inventory from the same publisher.

RESELLER

A RESELLER entry means the publisher has authorized a third party to sell their inventory through the ad system listed. The publisher has a relationship with an intermediary — another SSP, network, or header bidding wrapper — and that intermediary in turn has a relationship with the listed exchange. There is at least one extra hop in the supply chain.

RESELLER entries are legitimate and common

. Publishers use resellers for many valid reasons: demand aggregation, regional monetization, specialized ad formats (audio, CTV, native), or because a preferred header bidding wrapper routes demand through multiple exchanges. A high-quality publisher may legitimately have dozens of RESELLER entries.

Comparison at a Glance

Attribute DIRECT RESELLER
Contractual relationship Publisher ↔ Ad System Publisher ↔ Intermediary ↔ Ad System
Supply chain hops 1 (minimal) 2 or more
Buyer preference Strongly preferred Accepted, but lower priority
Typical CPM impact Higher Lower (more fees extracted)
sellers.json cross-check seller_type = PUBLISHER or BOTH seller_type = INTERMEDIARY or BOTH
Fraud risk Lower (shorter chain) Slightly higher (more parties)

How DSPs Use ads.txt

DSP validation is the process that gives ads.txt its fraud-prevention power. Compliant DSPs run this check on every bid request before deciding whether to bid. Here is the full validation flow:

① Bid Request Received

A bid request arrives at the DSP containing: the publisher's domain (site.domain), the selling SSP's domain (inferred from the supply chain or the OpenRTB app.publisher.domain field), and the publisher's seat ID (site.publisher.id).

↓

② Fetch the Publisher's ads.txt

The DSP fetches https://[publisher-domain]/ads.txt. In practice, DSPs crawl ads.txt files periodically (every 24–48 hours) and cache the results. Real-time fetching on every bid would be impractical at the scale of billions of daily requests.

↓

③ Parse & Match the Seller

The DSP searches the ads.txt file for a line where Field 1 (Ad System Domain) matches the SSP's domain and Field 2 (Publisher Account ID) matches the seat ID claimed in the bid request. Both fields must match exactly — the match is case-insensitive for the domain but case-sensitive for the account ID.

↓

④ Decision: Bid or Filter

If a matching entry is found: The seller is authorized. The DSP proceeds to evaluate the bid on price and targeting criteria.

If no matching entry exists: The seller is unauthorized. The DSP discards the bid request. No bid is placed, and the fraudulent (or merely unlisted) seller earns nothing.

↓

⑤ (Optional) Cross-Check with sellers.json

Advanced DSPs perform an additional verification: checking the SSP's sellers.json file to confirm that the seat ID maps to a seller of the appropriate type (PUBLISHER for DIRECT entries, INTERMEDIARY for RESELLER entries). This two-way check makes large-scale domain spoofing economically unviable.

Important: "No ads.txt" vs "Empty ads.txt"

A domain with no ads.txt file (HTTP 404) is treated differently from a domain with an empty ads.txt file (HTTP 200, empty body). Per the IAB spec, if no ads.txt is found, some DSPs still bid — treating the absence as "no restriction declared." An empty ads.txt file, however, explicitly authorizes no sellers — all bid requests from that domain should be rejected.

ads.txt vs app-ads.txt

When mobile app advertising became a significant part of the programmatic ecosystem, the IAB Tech Lab extended the ads.txt concept to mobile and CTV apps with app-ads.txt, published in 2019. While the file format is identical, the hosting and verification mechanisms differ significantly.

Attribute ads.txt app-ads.txt
Applies to Web / desktop domains Mobile apps, CTV apps, OTT
File location domain.com/ads.txt (root of publisher domain) developer-site.com/app-ads.txt (developer's registered website)

Frequently Asked Questions

Q What is ads.txt?

ads.txt (Authorized Digital Sellers) is an IAB Tech Lab standard introduced in 2017 that allows publishers to publicly declare which companies are authorized to sell their digital advertising inventory. Publishers host a plain-text file at the root of their domain (e.g., example.com/ads.txt), listing all authorized supply-side platforms (SSPs), ad exchanges, and resellers. DSPs fetch and parse this file to verify that sellers sending bid requests are actually authorized by the publisher.

Q Is ads.txt mandatory?

ads.txt is not legally mandated, but it is effectively required for full programmatic monetization. Major DSPs including Google DV360, The Trade Desk, and Amazon DSP now heavily filter or discount inventory from publishers without a valid ads.txt file. In practice, publishers without ads.txt lose access to premium programmatic demand, resulting in significantly lower fill rates and CPMs. The MRC (Media Rating Council) also considers ads.txt compliance a baseline requirement for accreditation.

Q How do I create an ads.txt file?

Creating an ads.txt file is straightforward:

1. Create a plain-text file named ads.txt (lowercase, no extension).
2. Contact each SSP, ad exchange, or ad network you work with and ask for your account's Publisher Account ID and their canonical Ad System Domain.
3. Add one line per authorized seller: AdSystemDomain, PublisherAccountID, DIRECT|RESELLER, CertificationAuthorityID
4. Upload the file to the web root of your domain so it's accessible at https://yourdomain.com/ads.txt.
5. Verify it's publicly accessible and correctly formatted.

Your SSPs will typically provide you the exact lines to add. Google AdSense and Ad Manager also have tools that auto-generate the correct ads.txt lines for your account.

Q What happens if ads.txt is missing?

If a domain returns HTTP 404 for /ads.txt, the IAB spec says compliant DSPs may still bid — treating the absence as "no restriction declared." However, many DSPs treat a missing ads.txt as a negative quality signal and apply bid dampening or exclusion filters.

If a domain has an ads.txt file but a seller is not listed in it, compliant DSPs must not purchase inventory from that unlisted seller. The most dangerous scenario is an accidental empty or malformed ads.txt: this can block all programmatic revenue while returning HTTP 200, making the problem difficult to diagnose without monitoring.

Q What is DIRECT vs RESELLER in ads.txt?

DIRECT means the publisher has a direct contractual relationship with the ad system listed. The publisher signed up for an account with that SSP or exchange and receives payment directly from them — no intermediary.

RESELLER means the publisher has authorized another entity to resell their inventory through the listed ad system — there is at least one intermediary in the supply chain. Both are legitimate, but DIRECT inventory commands higher CPMs because the supply chain is shorter, fees are lower, and buyer confidence is higher. For any given publisher, there should be fewer DIRECT entries (one per SSP they directly work with) and potentially many RESELLER entries.

Q How does ads.txt prevent ad fraud?

ads.txt's primary target is domain spoofing — a fraud technique where a fraudulent seller claims to be selling inventory from a premium publisher (such as a major newspaper) to extract inflated CPMs from DSPs. Before ads.txt, DSPs had no way to verify whether "New York Times inventory" being offered by some obscure SSP was genuine.

ads.txt solves this by giving publishers direct authorship over a whitelist of approved sellers. Because the file lives on the publisher's actual web server (which they control), only someone with legitimate access to that domain can modify it. A fraudster cannot add themselves to nytimes.com/ads.txt without access to the NYT's server. When a DSP receives a bid request claiming premium inventory from an unlisted seller, it rejects it immediately — making domain spoofing economically unviable at scale.
